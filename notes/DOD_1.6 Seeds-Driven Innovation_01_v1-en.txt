
VISH KRISHNAN: Seed-driven or supply-driven
innovation is the name we'll use to refer to approaches where
the drive for innovation, the push for innovation starts from the supply side.
In this case, you look at the new technological approaches
and capabilities that are emerging from our labs
like transistors, new algorithms, gene sequencing methods.
Gene editing models that we as a society are developing,
and ask what major problems could we solve now with these capabilities?
And we could do them much more efficiently and effectively
than the incumbent solutions that we have today.
In some cases, it does involve what is called applications innovation.
That is finding suitable applications that could actually benefit
from this new supply capability.
In other cases, it may even require some adaptation,
some recombination of these new found supply capabilities
evolving them to solve the problems at hand.
Most highly successful companies in this area like an Intel
or a Qualcomm that have unleashed the micro electronics
revolution, the transistor technology have taken this approach
where they have used the supply capability to find new applications
and solve them effectively.
Seeds-driven innovations, these supply-driven approaches
may look like a hammer in search of a nail.
That's one of their problems.
But in some cases, the bottleneck or constraining factor
is not really the needs identification that may actually be a number of well
understood needs that are captured.
But it's really solving them, and achieving
order of magnitude improvements that is essential
for adoption of these new innovations can actually
be done if we have compelling seeds, supply-driven capabilities.
It is true that we must tightly blend the seed-driven capabilities,
supply-driven capabilities with the market, and customer discovery,
and needs identification process needs in order to realize
a powerful innovation outcome.
