KARIM LAKHANI: Business architectures, the business model
you have, the operating model you have can help you win in the marketplace,
but also can become an Achilles heel as the market changes.
A great example of this is what happened to Blockbuster in the United States
when it came to video rentals.
So some of you may recall Blockbuster was a company that
was literally a blockbuster.
On every block, there was a Blockbuster video rental store.
And here was a business model that was based on charging members
a fee for renting DVDs or renting VHS.
And by the early '90s, Blockbuster led the US video rental market.
At its height in 2004, Blockbuster had more than 9,000 stores open worldwide.
Blockbuster purchased VHS tapes, and later DVDs for distribution,
and then rent them out for customers for three days.
And if you were late, they charged you a late fee.
In fact, Blockbuster made up to $800 million
per year in late fees, 16% of their revenue.
They had a very interesting business model and a very interesting operating
model.
However, over time, Blockbuster's business model and the operating model,
its business architecture collapsed in the face of Netflix.
And so they went from a peak price in the stock market
to then becoming bankrupt.
And what we're going to try to understand next
is how the business model and the operating model that Blockbuster
had ran right aground against Netflix, how
Netflix changed every aspect of video consumptions,
value creation, value capture, and operating model.
And we'll see how you as an entrepreneur entering the marketplace for technology
both need to think about not just your own business model and your operating
model, but also the potential competitors that exist,
and how either you are going to be differentiated from them,
or the threats you might face from your incumbents or your competitors
based on the logic of your business model and your operating model.
