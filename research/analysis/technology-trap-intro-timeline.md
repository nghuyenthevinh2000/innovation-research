# The Technology Trap: Introduction Timeline of Technology Disruptions

Source: Carl Benedikt Frey, *The Technology Trap: Capital, Labor, and Power in the Age of Automation* (Princeton University Press, 2019), Introduction.

## The framework

Frey splits "labor-saving" technology into two kinds. He builds on Acemoglu & Restrepo's model.

| Label | Meaning | Effect on workers |
|---|---|---|
| 🟢 **ENABLING** (augmenting) | Makes people more productive in existing tasks, or creates new jobs | Wages and jobs rise; little resistance |
| 🔴 **REPLACING** | Takes over tasks that labor used to do, making jobs and skills redundant | Displacement, falling wages, resistance and unrest |
| 🟡 **MIXED** | Replaces some workers and enables others. Usually the new jobs go to "another fellow" with different skills | Winners and losers at the same time |

Core thesis: how workers fare depends on the race between task replacement and new task creation, and on how easily displaced workers can move into the new jobs. When progress is replacing, hostility follows. When it is enabling and the gains are widely shared, people accept it.

## Timeline

### Era 0: Preindustrial ("The Great Stagnation", up to ~1700)
Innovation existed, but replacing technology was usually blocked by ruling elites who feared unrest.

| When | Technology | Label | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~10,000 years ago | Agriculture | n/a | Starting point of the book's survey (Ch. 1). The Introduction does not classify it. |
| 69–79 AD | Machinery to haul columns up the Capitoline Hill (Rome) | 🔴 REPLACING (blocked) | Emperor Vespasian refused it over employment concerns. |
| 1414 | Public streetlights (London) | 🟢 ENABLING | Created the lamplighter profession, which lasted about 500 years. |
| Late 1400s | Gutenberg's movable-type printing press | 🟡 MIXED (mostly benign) | Copyists rarely resisted. Most didn't earn a living from manuscripts, and those who did moved into short texts, binding, or book design. **Lesson: good alternative jobs mean little resistance.** |
| 1485 | Printing (Ottoman Empire) | n/a (political ban) | Sultan Bayezid II banned printing in Arabic for fear that literacy would weaken his rule. Literacy and growth suffered for a long time. |
| 1551 | Gig mill (cloth finishing) | 🔴 REPLACING (banned) | Prohibited in Britain because it saved so much labor. |
| 1589 | William Lee's stocking-frame knitting machine | 🔴 REPLACING (blocked) | Elizabeth I refused the patent, fearing unemployment. |
| 1600s | Automatic looms | 🔴 REPLACING (banned) | Banned in many European cities. Where they were adopted (e.g. Leiden), riots followed. |

### Era 1: First Industrial Revolution ("The Great Divergence", ~1750–1840s)
Nearly all the key technologies were replacing. The British government sided with industrialists instead of workers, which is one reason Britain industrialized first.

| When | Technology | Label | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early 1700s | Silk-throwing machines (from Piedmont, via Thomas Lombe's industrial espionage) | 🔴 REPLACING | Led to the first factories. |
| ~1750–1840 | Mechanized factory, cotton spinning machines | 🔴 REPLACING | Displaced the domestic system of skilled artisans. Machines were designed for children, who reached about 50% of the textile workforce by the 1830s ("the robots of the Industrial Revolution"). Real wages were flat or falling, food consumption didn't rise until the 1840s, people grew shorter, and profit rates doubled. |
| 1811–16 | (Reaction) Luddite risings | n/a | 12,000 troops were deployed, more than Wellington took to the Peninsular War in 1808. Many Luddites were jailed or transported to Australia. |
| 1800s | Power loom | 🟡 MIXED | Hand-loom weavers' incomes collapsed almost immediately. New jobs went to power-loom weavers, but their wages took decades to rise. |
| Late 1800s | Imported sewing machines (China) | 🔴 REPLACING (resisted) | Destroyed by native workers. This is one explanation for China's late industrialization. |

### Era 2: Second Industrial Revolution ("The Great Leveling", ~1870s–1970s, US-led)
Mostly enabling. Machine-tending jobs supported a large middle class, and after the 1870s workers rarely targeted machines.

| When | Technology | Label | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late 1800s | First electric streetlights with manual switches | 🟡 MIXED (de-skilling) | Made the job easier (no torches), but anyone could now do it, even schoolboys. "Simplification was merely a step toward automation." |
| 1890–1929 | Steel production technology | 🟢 ENABLING | 1929 output would have needed 1.25M workers with 1890 technology but only took 400k. Demand kept growing, so steel employment still **grew**. |
| (no date given) | Typewriter / office machines | 🟢 ENABLING | Created clerical jobs that hadn't existed before. |
| (no date given) | Elevator (manual) | 🟢 ENABLING | Created elevator-operator jobs and made skyscrapers possible. |
| (later) | Automatic elevator | 🔴 REPLACING | Eliminated the operator, even though we use elevators more than ever. |
| 1900s–1950 | Electricity, automobiles, mass production; radios, telephones, refrigerators | 🟢 ENABLING | Spawned huge new industries and pulled workers into better-paying city jobs. Wages rose at every level. |
| 1907–1927 | Substation-controlled electric streetlights | 🔴 REPLACING | Lamplighters were cut in large numbers. NYC lamplighters struck in 1907, and lamplighters in Verviers, Belgium rioted (the army was called in). The last NYC lamplighters left in 1927. |
| 1910 → | Electric washing machine (Alva J. Fisher's "Thor") and household appliances | 🔴 REPLACING (for laundresses) / 🟢 ENABLING (for households) | The number of laundresses peaked around 1910 and then fell. The "electric servant" freed households from drudgery. |
| (no date given) | Tractor | 🔴 REPLACING | Replaced farm horses. Leontief: "If horses could have… voted, what happened on farms might have been different." |
| 1933 | (Reaction) Nazi machinery restrictions | n/a | In Danzig, machinery needed government permission. "Never again must the worker be replaced by a machine." |

### Era 3: Computer revolution ("The Great Reversal", ~1965/1980s–present)
Automation is not a continuation of mechanization. It reverses it, replacing exactly the middle-income machine-tending jobs that mechanization had created.

| When | Technology | Label | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1965 | First electronic computers in offices | 🔴 REPLACING (warning) | Eric Hoffer warned that a skilled population "deprived of its sense and usefulness" would be "the ideal setup for an American Hitler." |
| 1980s → | Computerization and industrial robots (factories and offices) | 🔴 REPLACING (middle-skill) | Middle-income manufacturing and clerical jobs hollowed out. Prime-age men without college degrees lost most. The harm is local: joblessness, crime, mortality, less social mobility, and more populist voting. |
| 1980s → | Computer-aided design, Stata/Matlab, software jobs | 🟢 ENABLING (high-skill) | Made architects, engineers, and analysts more productive, and created new jobs (software engineers, database administrators). These went to "another fellow" with very different skills, an economy "designed by geniuses to be operated by other geniuses." |

### Era 4: The future (AI, Part 5)

| When | Technology | Label | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2010s → | AI, machine learning, machine vision, sensors, mobile robotics, 3-D printing, IoT | 🔴 Predominantly REPLACING | They will create some new tasks, but Frey expects them to keep shrinking the already weakened middle class. Most Americans would vote to restrict automation, and it could become a populist target the way globalization already has. |

## Pattern at a glance

```
Preindustrial      Industrial Rev.     2nd Industrial Rev.     Computer / AI age
(-> 1700)          (1750-1840s)        (1870s-1970s)           (1980s -> )
REPLACING          REPLACING           ENABLING                REPLACING
but BLOCKED        and ENFORCED        and ACCEPTED            -> backlash risk
by elites          by the state        (wages rise for all)    (middle class shrinks)
```

## Key takeaways

1. **Replacing technology causes resistance.** Opposition to it was the rule rather than the exception until the late 1800s.
2. **Outside job options matter.** Copyists accepted printing because they had alternatives. Weavers smashed looms because they didn't.
3. **The short run can be a lifetime.** Britain's real wages didn't improve for 60–80 years after the technology boom of the 1760s.
4. **Today mirrors the Industrial Revolution:** middle-income jobs are disappearing, wages are stagnating, and the gains go to capital (the "Gates paradox").
5. **Politics decides adoption.** Elites blocked replacing technology when unrest threatened them and enforced it when they stood to gain, as with Britain's "chimney aristocrats."
