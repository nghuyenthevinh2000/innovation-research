# The Technology Trap: Labor-Replacing Moments, Winners vs. Losers

Source: Carl Benedikt Frey, *The Technology Trap* (Princeton UP, 2019). The moments are the ones flagged in the Introduction, with details added from the chapters cited in each entry. Companion file: `technology-trap-intro-timeline.md`.

**How to read each entry**

- **Winners**: who gained (owners of capital, the state, consumers, new-skill workers).
- **Losers**: whose skills or income were made redundant.
- **Before**: what each side did while the technology was being introduced or debated.
- **After**: what happened once it was adopted, or blocked.
- **Outcome**: which side held political power, and so won.

---

## Era 0: Preindustrial. The elites side with the losers

### 1. Column-hauling machine, Rome (69–79 AD) *(Intro; Ch. 1)*

- **Winners (potential):** the Roman state, which would have saved a huge transport bill, and the inventor.
- **Losers (potential):** the thousands of laborers who hauled columns to the Capitoline Hill.
- **Before:** An inventor offered Emperor Vespasian the device. Vespasian refused: *"How will it be possible for me to feed the populace?"* (He rewarded the inventor but didn't use the machine.)
- **After:** Never adopted. Keeping workers busy was treated as insurance against unrest. (A related story: Tiberius executed the inventor of unbreakable glass for the same reason.)
- **Outcome:** **Blocked.** For the ruler, the risk of unrest outweighed the savings.

### 2. The gig mill (banned 1551, unbanned 1809) *(Intro; Ch. 1, 3, 5)*

- **Winners:** cloth manufacturers. One man and two boys could do the work of 18 men and 6 boys.
- **Losers:** shearers and croppers, members of one of the strongest woollen guilds.
- **Before:** Banned by statute in 1551. Charles I issued another proclamation against it about a century later, which shows people kept using it. The shearers' guild blocked it in the west of England for decades through petitions and violence.
- **After:** Resistance in Wiltshire and Somerset collapsed once Gloucester shearers adopted the mills and took their business. Workers petitioned Parliament for a decade to enforce the old ban, and in 1809 Parliament repealed it instead. That helped trigger the Yorkshire Luddite riots (see #7).
- **Outcome:** Blocked for about 250 years, then **forced through** once competition between towns weakened the guilds.

### 3. William Lee's stocking-frame knitting machine (1589) *(Intro; Ch. 1)*

- **Winners (potential):** the inventor William Lee, and hosiery producers.
- **Losers:** the hosiers' guild, whose hand-knitting skills it would make redundant.
- **Before:** The guild lobbied against it. Elizabeth I refused the patent: *"Consider thou what the invention could do to my poor subjects… depriving them of employment, thus making them beggars."*
- **After:** The guild's opposition was so intense that Lee had to leave England. Two centuries later, the stocking frame was the main machine the Nottinghamshire Luddites smashed (see #7).
- **Outcome:** **Blocked** by the Crown, siding with the guild.

### 4. Automatic looms and other machines, continental Europe (1600s–early 1700s) *(Intro; Ch. 1)*

- **Winners (potential):** merchant manufacturers.
- **Losers:** hand weavers, needle-makers, bucket-makers, and boatmen.
- **Before:** Many European cities issued edicts against automatic looms. Germany banned them entirely from 1685 to 1726. In England, the Privy Council ordered a needle-making machine abandoned in 1623 and its needles destroyed, and Charles I banned the casting of buckets.
- **After:** **Leiden riots, 1620**, where the looms were adopted. In 1705 the Fulda boatmen smashed the steam-powered boat of Denis Papin, a pioneer of steam power. Papin died a pauper.
- **Outcome:** **Blocked.** Rulers feared that angry workers would turn against the government.

## Era 1: First Industrial Revolution. The state sides with the winners

### 5. The British state switches sides (1688 → 1769 → 1779) *(Ch. 3)*

- **Winners:** a new class of industrialists, the "chimney aristocrats," and Britain's position in trade and war.
- **Losers:** the craft guilds and domestic (home-based) artisans.
- **Before:** After the Glorious Revolution (1688) the guilds weakened. Patent records show the shift: only 2% of patents filed in 1663–1750 claimed to save labor, and that share rose fourfold in 1750–1800.
- **After:** **1769: destroying machinery became a capital offense.** After the 1779 Lancashire riots, a resolution said that destroying the machines "would only be the means of transferring them to another [country]… to the detriment of the trade of Britain."
- **Outcome:** **Enforced.** Competition with other nations now looked like a bigger threat than unrest at home.

### 6. Mechanized cotton factory and spinning machines (~1750–1840) *(Intro; Ch. 4–5)*

- **Winners:** factory owners, whose profit share of national income doubled. Consumers, who got cheaper textiles. Children, as cheap and controllable labor.
- **Losers:** adult male artisans in the domestic system. Child workers too, in health and dignity: children made up about 50% of the textile workforce by the 1830s.
- **Before:** Machines were deliberately designed for children to operate ("the robots of the Industrial Revolution").
- **After:** **"Engels' pause."** Real wages were flat or falling until about 1840. Food consumption didn't rise until the 1840s, people grew shorter, and life expectancy in Manchester and Glasgow was about 10 years below the national average. Words like "working class," "strike," "Luddite," and "communism" entered the language, and the period ended with the *Communist Manifesto*.
- **Outcome:** Winners took the gains for about seven decades. From 1840 to 1900 the gains spread more widely: real wages rose 123% while output per worker rose 90%. Steam-powered machines needed skilled adult operators, and the Factory Acts made child labor more expensive.

### 7. The Luddite risings (1811–1816) *(Intro; Ch. 3, 5, 11)*

- **Winners:** mill and frame owners, and Parliament's pro-free-trade majority.
- **Losers:** framework knitters in Nottinghamshire, croppers in Yorkshire, and cotton weavers in Lancashire.
- **Before:** Workers petitioned Parliament, which held committee hearings and did nothing. The 1812 committee's position was "no interference of the legislature with the freedom of trade." The new prime minister, Lord Liverpool, argued that relief would only slow workers' move into other jobs.
- **During:** At least 100 attacks destroyed about 1,000 of the 25,000 stocking frames, causing around £100k in damage. Crowds smashed only the machines that threatened jobs and left the others alone. Local communities protected them.
- **After:** **12,000 troops** were deployed, more than Wellington took to the Peninsular War. **More than 30 Luddites were hanged** in 1812–13, and others were jailed or transported to Australia.
- **Outcome:** **Losers crushed.** They had no vote (property ownership was required) and no political power. Frey: if the Luddites had won, the Industrial Revolution would probably not have started in Britain.

### 8. Power loom vs. hand-loom weavers (early to mid-1800s) *(Intro; Ch. 5)*

- **Winners:** mill owners, consumers, and eventually power-loom weavers, though their wages took decades to rise.
- **Losers:** hand-loom weavers, formerly "labour's aristocrats." The historian Duncan Bythell called this "the largest case of… technological unemployment in our recent economic history."
- **Before:** Defenders of the machines (Baines, Ure) argued that machinery complemented labor and that cheaper goods would compensate workers.
- **After:** Weavers' earnings fell to subsistence. Unemployment was 60% among Stockport weavers in 1816 and 69% in Darwen a decade later, and 5,000 weavers were out of work in Glasgow. Some migrated to find work elsewhere.
- **Outcome:** The new jobs went to "another fellow," not to the people who were displaced.

### 9. Captain Swing riots: threshing machines (1830) *(Ch. 5, 11)*

- **Winners:** farmers and landowners who adopted the machines.
- **Losers:** rural farm laborers who depended on winter threshing work.
- **Before:** Threshing machines spread across parishes. The probability of riots rose where they were adopted (Caprettini & Voth).
- **During:** More than 2,000 riots, and 492 machines destroyed between September and November 1830. Riots spread when people in one parish heard about riots in neighboring ones.
- **After:** The army and militias were sent in. **252 death sentences** were passed, though some were commuted to transportation to Australia or New Zealand. **Political twist:** the unrest helped the reform-friendly Whigs win the 1831 election, which led to the **Great Reform Act of 1832** and began Britain's road to democracy.
- **Outcome:** The rioters lost the fight over machines, but their unrest helped start democratic reform.

### 10. Imported sewing machines, China (late 1800s) *(Intro)*

- **Losers:** native craftworkers. **Action:** they destroyed the machines.
- **Outcome:** Resistance worked, and lasting resistance like this is one explanation for **China's late industrialization**.

## Era 2: Second Industrial Revolution. Replacing technology is the exception, and backlash is mostly verbal

### 11. Electric streetlights vs. lamplighters (1907–1927) *(Intro)*

- **Winners:** city residents (cheaper, brighter light), gas and electric utilities, and substation staff. One substation worker could switch on thousands of lamps in seconds.
- **Losers:** lamplighters, a profession that had existed since 1414 (600 of them in NYC in 1900).
- **Before:** Early electric lamps still had manual switches, so the job got easier but less skilled: schoolboys could do it. "Simplification was merely a step toward automation."
- **During:** On **April 24, 1907, NYC lamplighters struck** and most of Manhattan's 25,000 gas lamps went unlit. Police tried to light them (many couldn't climb the posts), and boys in Harlem kept turning them back off. In **Verviers, Belgium**, lamplighters attacked replacement crews and raided police headquarters, and the army was called in.
- **After:** Substation control cut jobs in large numbers. The last two NYC gas lamplighters quit in 1927, and the Lamplighters Union ended with them.
- **Outcome:** **Replaced**, but many lamplighters probably found safer, better-paying work in a booming economy.

### 12. Electric washing machine vs. laundresses (1910 →) *(Intro)*

- **Winners:** households, freed from drudgery by the "electric servant."
- **Losers:** laundresses working in private households. Their numbers peaked around 1910, the year Alva J. Fisher patented the Thor washing machine, and then fell.
- **Reaction:** None organized. Frey uses this case to show that extrapolating from past employment trends (rather than watching the technology) would have missed the decline.

### 13. "The Machinery Question" returns (1927–1940) and Nazi restrictions (1933) *(Intro; Ch. 7)*

- **Before (US):** Labor Secretary James J. Davis started the "technological unemployment" debate in 1927. The Depression amplified it into House hearings, radio shows, and films. In 1940 Roosevelt said America must find "jobs faster than invention can take them away."
- **Before (Germany):** In 1933 the Danzig Senate required government permission to install factory machinery, with heavy fines or forced closure for breaking the rule. Labor Front leader Alfred von Hodenberg: *"Never again must the worker be replaced by a machine."*
- **After (US):** The debate faded with World War II and full employment. There were no machine bans.
- **Outcome:** Where enabling technology and good jobs were plentiful (the US), worry stayed verbal. Where politics was radicalized (Nazi Germany), restrictions came back.

### 14. Automation anxiety, 1950s–60s: computers, telephone operators, longshoremen *(Intro; Ch. 7–8)*

- **Winners:** firms, and executives and supervisors able to run automated systems.
- **Losers:** telephone operators and longshoremen (dockworkers). Elevator operators, too, once automatic elevators arrived (Intro).
- **Before:** In 1960 Kennedy called automation both "bright with hope" and a "dark menace." In 1965 Eric Hoffer warned of "an American Hitler." Johnson's National Commission on Technology, Automation, and Economic Progress (report published 1966) found automation was not the cause of unemployment, but still recommended making the government an employer of last resort.
- **During:** The **1968 telephone operators' strike** of 160,440 workers had almost no effect: "Automation Keeps Struck Phone System Working."
- **After:** The longshoremen's union got only compensation and retraining when containerization made its members' skills obsolete, and its clout shrank with its membership.
- **Outcome:** Unions accepted mechanization in exchange for higher wages and a welfare and education system, which Frey calls the 20th-century social contract. But a union whose members' skills are automated loses its bargaining power.

## Era 3: Computer and robot age. The losers turn to the ballot box

### 15. Computerization and industrial robots (1980s–2016) *(Intro; Ch. 9–11)*

- **Winners:** college-educated workers in skilled cities (software, design, and data jobs), capital owners, and consumers (cheaper cars and goods).
- **Losers:** middle-income factory and office machine-tenders, especially prime-age men without degrees, in Rust Belt communities.
  - Ohio lost 350,000 factory jobs after 2000, and median income fell from $57.7k (2000) to $49.3k (2015). Ohio has the second-most factory robots of any state, after Michigan.
  - Only 50% of Americans born in 1980 out-earn their parents, compared with 90% of those born in 1940.
- **Before:** Unlike the Luddites, workers did not smash machines. They moved into lower-paid jobs (often in health care) or left the workforce. Union membership and political voice fell away.
- **After:** The harm concentrated in communities: rising joblessness, crime, suicide, alcohol-related deaths, collapsing marriage rates, lower social mobility. Voters responded at the polls: robot exposure is linked to support for Trump in 2016. Frey, Berger & Chen estimate that **Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin would have gone to Clinton** without the growth in robots after 2012.
- **Outcome:** Losers are using votes rather than hammers. Populism targets globalization so far, and automation could be next.

### 16. AI and the "New Luddites" (2017 → future) *(Intro; Ch. 11–13)*

- **Winners (projected):** AI and robotics firms, highly skilled workers, and consumers.
- **Losers (projected):** the already shrinking middle class, now including workers in sectors like services that are shielded from globalization but not from automation.
- **Before (signals already visible):**
  - In a 2017 Pew survey, **85%** of Americans supported restricting automation to hazardous jobs.
  - **About 70%** of respondents with a high school education or less wanted limits on how many jobs firms can give to machines, compared with about 40% of college graduates.
  - Andrew Yang planned a 2020 presidential run on a value-added tax on companies that use automation.
- **After (Frey's warning):** If the trend continues for decades, as it did during Engels' pause, "there is nothing that shields automation from becoming a target." Social media spreads discontent much faster than markets and fairs did for the Swing rioters.
- **Frey's prescription (Ch. 13):** wage insurance, relocation vouchers, tax credits, education and early-childhood investment, and zoning reform. These ease the adjustment without stopping the technology.

## Cross-cutting pattern

| Moment | Who held political power | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rome, Elizabethan England, 1600s Europe | Monarchs and landed elites (fearing unrest) | **Losers protected**, technology blocked |
| Britain 1769–1830s | Industrialists and Parliament (fearing foreign competition) | **Winners protected**, rioters hanged or transported |
| China, late 1800s | Workers able to resist | Technology blocked, late industrialization |
| US 1900s–1970s | Unions plus a mass middle class; mostly enabling technology | Replacement accepted, displaced workers compensated |
| US 1980s–today | Fragmented working class with less union voice | Losers turn to populist voting |
| AI future | Unknown | Depends on policy choices made now |

**Counterexample (not replacing in practice):** the Gutenberg printing press, late 1400s. Copyists rarely resisted because they had alternatives: shorter texts, binding, and design. Frey's rule follows from this. **Resistance depends on displaced workers' outside job options**, not on the technology alone.
