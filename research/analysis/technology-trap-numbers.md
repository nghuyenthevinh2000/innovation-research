# The Technology Trap: Before → After Numbers (Cause → Outcome)

Source: Carl Benedikt Frey, *The Technology Trap* (Princeton UP, 2019). Chapter tags (Intro, Ch. 1–13) point to where each number appears.
Companion files: `technology-trap-intro-timeline.md`, `technology-trap-replacing-moments.md`.

**Rule for this file:** a number is kept only if it shows a change: before vs. after a technology arrived, or with vs. without it. Each episode is written as a chain:

> **Cause** (technology or policy) → **Mechanism** (what it did to work) → **Outcome** (the measured before → after)

Legend: 🔴 labor-replacing · 🟢 labor-enabling · ⚖️ distribution of gains · 🔮 projection (no "after" yet)

## 1. Preindustrial: what changed when the guilds lost power

**Cause:** the Glorious Revolution (1688) and competition between towns weakened the craft guilds → **Mechanism:** inventors no longer had to avoid labor-saving designs → **Outcome:** labor-saving invention took off.

| Metric | Before | After | Change | Causal reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Share of English patents claiming to save labor *(Ch. 3)* | 2% (1663–1750) | ~8% (1750–1800) | **4×** | Once guilds lost the power to block machines, inventors switched to replacing technology. |
| Labor needed for a unit of cloth finishing, with the gig mill *(Ch. 1)* | 18 men + 6 boys | 1 man + 2 boys | **−~88% of workers** | Why it was banned in 1551, and why repealing the ban in 1809 helped trigger the Luddite riots of 1811–16. |

## 2. First Industrial Revolution (Britain): replacing technology and "Engels' pause"

**Cause:** the water frame, spinning machines, and the power loom → **Mechanism:** adult artisans were replaced by machines operated by children → **Outcome:** output soared, wages stalled, and capital captured the gains.

### 2a. Machine-level effect
| Metric | Before | After | Change | Causal reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 🔴 Labor cost of spinning, with Arkwright's water frame *(Ch. 4)* | 100 (index) | ~33 | **−2/3** | Direct replacement of spinners. |
| 🔴 Overall cost of coarse cotton production *(Ch. 4)* | 100 (index) | 80 | **−20%** | Cheaper output was the gain to consumers. |
| Cotton industry's share of GDP *(Ch. 4)* | Marginal (1750) | 8% (1830) | — | The winning sector became Britain's largest. |

### 2b. Economy-wide: who got the gains (1760–1840)
| Metric | Before | After | Change | Causal reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ⚖️ Output per worker *(Ch. 5)* | 1780 | 1840 | **+46%** | Machines raised productivity... |
| ⚖️ Real weekly wages *(Ch. 5)* | 1780 | 1840 | **+12%** | ...but workers received about a quarter of it. |
| ⚖️ Average working hours *(Ch. 5)* | 1760 | 1830 | **+20%** | Higher weekly pay came from longer hours, so **real hourly pay fell** for many. |
| ⚖️ Top 5% income share *(Ch. 5)* | 21% (1759) | 37% (1867) | **+16 pts (~doubled)** | The gains concentrated at the top. |
| ⚖️ Profit rate / profit share of national income *(Intro; Ch. 5)* | Early 1800s | ~1840 | **~2×** | Industrialists captured the surplus. |
| Urban share (towns >5,000 people) *(Ch. 5)* | 21% (1750) | 45% (1850) | **+24 pts** | Displaced workers had to move to factory towns, into worse health conditions (life expectancy about 10 years below the national average in Manchester and Glasgow). |

### 2c. Hand-loom weavers: from "labour's aristocrats" to mass unemployment
| Metric | Before | After | Change | Causal reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 🔴 Weavers' status *(Ch. 5)* | "Labour's aristocrats" (pre-power loom) | Earnings at subsistence level | — | The power loom made their skill worthless. |
| 🔴 Weaver unemployment, Stockport *(Ch. 5)* | Employed craftsmen | 60% (1816) | — | Mass redundancy. |
| 🔴 Hand-loom weaver unemployment, Darwen *(Ch. 5)* | — | 69% (~1826) | Still high a decade later | The displacement was **persistent**, not cyclical. |
| 🔴 Textiles' share of Northamptonshire's economy *(Ch. 5)* | 11% (1777) | 1% (1851) | **−10 pts** | Regions that lost to the factory towns de-industrialized. |

### 2d. Replacing technology → unrest
| Metric | Without machine | With machine | Change | Causal reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 🔴 Probability of a Captain Swing riot (1830), parishes without vs. with threshing machines (Caprettini & Voth) *(Ch. 5)* | Baseline | ~1.5× | **+~50%** | Adopting replacing technology **caused** more riots. |

### 2e. The turnaround: technology became enabling
**Cause:** larger steam-powered machines and the Factory Acts → **Mechanism:** machines now needed skilled adult operators instead of children → **Outcome:** wages caught up with productivity.

| Metric | Engels' pause (1780–1840) | After (1840–1900) | Causal reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⚖️ Output per worker | +46% | +90% | Productivity growth sped up... |
| ⚖️ Real wages | +12% | **+123%** | ...and wages now grew **faster** than output. Workers stopped smashing machines. |
| 🟢 Rail social savings, passenger travel (% of GDP) *(Ch. 4)* | 5% (1865) | 14% (1912) | Enabling infrastructure spread the gains widely. |

## 3. Second Industrial Revolution (US): enabling technology and "The Great Leveling"

### 3a. Productivity rose and jobs still grew
**Cause:** better steel technology → **Mechanism:** each worker produced more, while demand grew even faster → **Outcome:** no displacement.

| Metric | Before | After | Change | Causal reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 🟢 Workers needed for 1929's steel tonnage (Harry Jerome) *(Intro)* | 1.25M with 1890 technology | 400k with 1929 technology | **−68% per ton** | Yet steel **employment grew**. This is the textbook enabling case. |

### 3c. The automobile

## 4. Computer and robot age (US): replacing technology again, "The Great Reversal"

### 4a. Productivity rose and pay decoupled
**Cause:** computers and robots automated routine middle-skill tasks → **Mechanism:** replacement outpaced the creation of new tasks → **Outcome:** a second "Engels' pause."

| Metric | Before | After | Change | Causal reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ⚖️ Labor productivity vs. hourly compensation *(Ch. 9)* | Grew together (postwar) | Productivity **8×** faster (since 1979) | — | Parallel to Britain 1780–1840 (46% vs. 12%). |
| ⚖️ Labor share of income *(Ch. 9)* | ~64% (postwar) | ~58% (post-2008) | **−6 pts** | Capital captured the gains. |
| ⚖️ Children out-earning their parents *(Intro)* | 90% (born 1940) | 50% (born 1980) | **−40 pts** | The broken escalator. |
| 🟢 Price of computing *(Ch. 9)* | $487,000 (ENIAC) | ~$4,000 (top IBM PC, early 1980s) | **−99%** | Cheap computing made automation economical. |

### 4b. Direct replacement
| Metric | Before | After | Change | Causal reading |

## 7. How long the pain lasted, across eras

**Question:** how long after a technology wave starts does the affected group wait before it shares in the gains?
Two different clocks are tracked, because they measure different things:
- **Economy-wide pause:** years during which productivity rises but typical workers' real wages or living standards don't.
- **Individual adjustment:** how long a displaced worker takes to recover.

### 7a. Economy-wide pause
| Era | Technology wave | Pause starts | Pause ends | Duration | Evidence for the end point |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preindustrial (to ~1700) | Gig mill, stocking frame, automatic looms | — | — | **None** | Replacing technology was **blocked**, so displaced workers had no pause. Society paid instead through slow growth. *(Ch. 1, 3)* |
| 1st Industrial Revolution (Britain) | Mechanized factory, spinning machines, power loom | ~1770s (technology boom of the 1760s) | 1840s | **~70 years** ("some seven decades") | Food consumption and living standards rose only in the 1840s. Then 1840–1900: wages +123% vs. output +90%. *(Intro; Ch. 5)* |
| 2nd Industrial Revolution (US) | Electricity, cars, tractors, mass production | — | — | **~0 years** at the economy-wide level | Wages rose at every level: manufacturing pay +50% (1900–28), top-decile share −10 pts (1913–48). Displacement existed (−5.7M farm jobs) but workers were *pulled* into better jobs. *(Ch. 8, 11)* |
| Computer / robot age (US) | Computers, industrial robots | ~1979–80 | **Not ended** at the time of writing (2019) | **~40 years and counting** | Productivity has grown 8× faster than pay since 1979. Non-college men's real wages have fallen since the 1980s. The labor share fell 64% → 58%. *(Intro; Ch. 9)* |
| AI (future) | Machine learning, vision, robotics | 🔮 | 🔮 | 🔮 | Frey: "the next three decades must not mirror the past three." *(Intro)* |

### 7b. Individual adjustment (within an era)
| Era | Group | Recovery time | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st Industrial Revolution | Hand-loom weavers → power-loom weavers | Incomes fell "almost immediately". It "took decades" for power-loom weavers' wages to rise. Weaver unemployment was still 69% (Darwen) a decade after Stockport's 60% in 1816. | Intro; Ch. 5 |
| 2nd Industrial Revolution | Chicago garment cutters (1921–25) | **5.6 months** on average. 12.9% still jobless after a year. **90% of those over 45** never recovered their previous pay. | Ch. 7 |
| 2nd Industrial Revolution | 1930s "machinery question" (an anxiety, not a measured pause) | ~1927 → 1941: ended with WWII full employment | Ch. 7 |
| Computer age | Pennsylvania workers displaced in the early 1980s | Earnings still **−25% six years later**. Life expectancy −1.5 years, a permanent loss. | Ch. 10 |
| Computer age | Typesetters (1979–89) | Jobs −56%, median wage −16%, with no recovery reported | Ch. 9 |

### 7c. Reading the pattern
```
Economy-wide pause (years)
Preindustrial  |  (blocked: no pause, but no growth either)
1st Ind. Rev.  |██████████████████████████████████  ~70
2nd Ind. Rev.  |  ~0  (enabling: wages rose with productivity)
Computer age   |████████████████████ ~40 → still open in 2019
```

- **The data do not show the pause getting steadily shorter.** The sequence is 70 → ~0 → 40+, and the last number is **still running** (the book was written in 2019). In statistical terms it's "right-censored": we only know it has lasted *at least* 40 years. So the computer-age pause may end up close to, or longer than, Engels' pause.
- **The pause length tracks the type of technology, not how recent it is.** Replacing waves (1st Industrial Revolution, computers) produced long pauses. The enabling wave (2nd Industrial Revolution) produced essentially none. That matches Frey's central argument.
- **What ended the last pause:** technology turned enabling (larger steam machines needed skilled adult operators), and wages caught up with productivity. Frey argues there's no automatic equivalent today. Whether the current pause ends depends on new tasks for labor and on policy (Ch. 13).
- **Within every era, older displaced workers pay the most:** the 90% of garment cutters over 45 who never recovered their pay, and the Pennsylvania workers still −25% after six years. For them, "the short run can be a lifetime."

## Side-by-side: the two replacing eras

| Metric | Britain, 1780–1840 (Engels' pause) | US, 1979–2016 (the Great Reversal) |
|---|---|---|
| Productivity vs. wages | +46% vs. +12% | Productivity 8× faster than pay |

| What changed it last time | Technology turned enabling: wages +123% vs. output +90% (1840–1900) | Not yet. Frey argues this depends on policy choices made now. |
